## Core ideas to reuse as process checks

### Psychology and process

Elder's central structure is three-legged: individual psychology, a logical method for analyzing the market, and money management (Introduction and chs. 2, 5, 8, PDF pp. 14–23, 25–29, 40–45). A strong setup is not enough if the trader changes rules under stress or risks too much. His durable process recommendations are to define entry, invalidation and exit before entry; record the reason for each trade and all adjustments; preserve before/after charts; include commissions and slippage in results; review patterns of execution and emotion; and stop or reduce activity when loss of discipline is detected (chs. 7–11, 15, 46, 48, PDF pp. 37–55, 69–73, 273–289).

The “manage rather than forecast” principle (ch. 17, PDF pp. 80–83) is useful as a reporting discipline: describe observable conditions, the conditional path, invalidation and what would change the read. It does not prove technical analysis has predictive power. The book repeatedly urges skepticism toward gurus, secret systems, overfit backtests and certainty (chs. 5–6, 14, 24, 46–47, PDF pp. 25–36, 64–68, 130–134, 273–283).

### Chart reading and level marking

Use this as a transparent annotation workflow, not a mechanical signal:

1. State instrument, venue, session convention, timeframe and chart scale. Mark only completed bars. Identify the visible swing highs/lows and classify the chart as an uptrend (successive higher highs and higher lows), downtrend (lower lows and lower highs), range, or transition (chs. 18, 20; PDF pp. 84–89, 95–102).
2. Mark support and resistance as **zones** around repeated congestion edges, not as falsely precise single-tick lines. Label the zone's upper/lower boundaries, source touches, timeframe, date/price anchors, role (support/resistance), and whether price accepted, rejected or closed through it. Elder says congestion edges represent more participants than isolated panic tails; he also describes role reversal after a break. His proposed strength factors are duration, number of tests, vertical size and volume (ch. 19, PDF pp. 90–95). Treat these as descriptive context, not calibrated scoring weights.
3. Draw trendlines through the edges of congestion and meaningful swing points, and record the points used. Show the line's slope/timeframe and distinguish a provisional two-touch line from later confirmation. Do not move anchors retrospectively to make the line fit. Channels can frame context and possible reaction areas; do not call a channel edge a guaranteed reversal or target (chs. 20–21, 45; PDF pp. 95–110, 264–272).
4. Describe a pattern only after stating its boundaries and completion status. Separate candidate, trigger/confirmation, invalidation and any measured-move hypothesis. Elder covers head-and-shoulders, rectangles, flags, triangles, double tops/bottoms and gaps (chs. 22–23, PDF pp. 110–129). A pattern's visual label does not establish probability or edge; failed breakouts and contradictory patterns belong in the analysis.
5. Compare at least two timeframes. Elder's “factor of five” is a heuristic; for his swing example, weekly context constrains daily setup and a lower timeframe may help with entry. It is not a universal requirement and should be adapted to the user's horizon and instrument session (chs. 20, 36, 43; PDF pp. 100–102, 207–208, 252–260).
6. Use volume/open interest only if the provider's units, venue, contract and rollover meaning are known. State whether volume is spot, futures, per-venue or aggregate. Do not infer who traded, aggressor direction, “smart money,” accumulation, or new long/short positions from OHLCV alone. Volume and OI are context, not independent votes with price-derived indicators (chs. 32–35, PDF pp. 182–201).

### Indicators and regimes

The book distinguishes lagging/trend-following tools (moving averages, MACD, Directional System) from oscillators (Momentum/ROC, Williams %R, Stochastic, RSI, Elder-ray, Force Index) and breadth/sentiment measures (chs. 24, 28, PDF pp. 130–135, 157–181). The valuable caution is conditional use: trend tools can whipsaw in ranges; overbought/oversold oscillators can stay extreme in strong trends. Divergences are hypotheses requiring a causal, completed-pivot definition; confirmation can arrive late and apparent divergences can fail. MACD, moving averages, oscillators, trendlines and patterns all reuse price history, so do not count them as independent confirmation.

Selected source-specific constructions: EMA slope as broad trend context (ch. 25, pp. 135–142); classic MACD 12/26 with 9 signal and histogram difference (ch. 26, pp. 142–150); Elder-ray Bull Power = high minus EMA and Bear Power = low minus EMA (ch. 41, pp. 237–243); Force Index = volume × close-to-close price change, with 2- and 13-period smoothing examples (ch. 42, pp. 244–251). These are descriptions of the source, not plugin defaults or validated BTC/India parameters. Verify formulas, data adjustment, candle close and venue volume before implementing any indicator.

### Triple Screen (source method, unvalidated for these instruments)

Chapter 43 (PDF pp. 252–260) proposes: (1) identify the “tide” on a higher timeframe with a trend-following indicator; (2) use an intermediate-timeframe pullback/oscillator against that trend to find a setup; and (3) use a lower-timeframe stop-entry trigger in the higher-timeframe direction. The worked example uses weekly MACD-Histogram slope, daily Force Index/Elder-ray/Stochastic, and a trailing stop entry. This is a coherent way to separate regime, setup and trigger. The exact factor-of-five relation, one-tick offsets, indicator lengths, very tight stops and results are historical choices and must not be copied to BTC perpetuals/options or Indian derivatives without contract-aware implementation and out-of-sample testing.

### Risk, expectancy and exits

The strongest transferable principle is that sizing follows a pre-defined loss budget and a logical invalidation point; it does not follow confidence or nominal notional. The book distinguishes positive expectancy from money management (chs. 46–47, PDF pp. 273–283): calculate expected outcome net of fees, spread, slippage and other carrying costs; if the edge is unestablished, position sizing cannot create it. The source argues for survival first, warns against martingale/doubling after losses and averaging down, and emphasizes drawdown asymmetry. Its own proposed 2% maximum risk per trade and 6–8% monthly stop are **not** adopted as defaults; the author presents broad numerical claims without modern BTC/India evidence or full risk-model specification (chs. 10, 47, pp. 49–51, 278–283).

For any conditional plan, calculate risk from entry to stop using contract multiplier/lot size and include fees, spread, likely slippage/gap, funding/carry and correlated open exposure. Confirm the stop is valid for the venue and order type; a stop price does not guarantee a fill at that price. For options, include premium, expiry, Greeks, volatility, liquidity and short-option tail risk; do not transfer stock/futures examples mechanically. If the stop-based loss exceeds the user's stated risk budget or cannot be estimated from fresh data, show WAIT/no trade. Never infer a 2% user preference from this book.

Chapter 48 (PDF pp. 284–289) recommends deciding the exit plan before entry, moving stops only to reduce risk, avoiding emotional profit counting, using signals consistently, taking profits only under the chosen rule, and reviewing a before/after chart and journal. The “break-even” and “protect 50% of paper profits” examples are source heuristics, not universal rules; execution gaps and whipsaw risk remain.

## Source-specific limitations and errors to disclose

- The work is from 1993 and discusses open-outcry floors, old U.S. contracts, historical commissions, exchange practices, 1990s software and old stock-market breadth feeds. Its assertions about institutional/professional behavior, who sets opens/closes, market liquidity, futures insiders, slippage and market crowd composition should not be treated as current universal fact.
- Many deterministic claims (e.g. high volume “confirms,” divergence as a “strongest” signal, channels containing 90–95% of prices, cycle noise percentages, repeated reversal patterns, and particular win/return/risk expectations) are not independently established by this reading. Preserve them as Elder's historical heuristics; do not present as a current empirical result.
- The 2% rule is framed as tested and near-universal, but the book does not give enough sample, instrument, cost or methodology detail to transfer it. It is not a recommendation for this user.
- The psychological account includes broad psychoanalytic and addiction analogies (chs. 7–11, PDF pp. 37–55). Use only behavioral prompts—impulsivity, denial, revenge trading, inability to stop and recordkeeping. Do not diagnose the user or equate ordinary trading losses with addiction. Refer crisis or gambling-harm concerns to appropriate support if they arise.
- Some OCR and chart captions are damaged. The explicit chapter map and formulas above were checked against the source text and selected images; do not quote garbled OCR. Use source paraphrase, exact chapter and physical PDF page instead.
- The book itself says methods evolve (Afterword, PDF pp. 290–293). A historical source is not a substitute for current Delta Exchange India or Upstox data, current contract specifications, current regulations, or a tested strategy.
