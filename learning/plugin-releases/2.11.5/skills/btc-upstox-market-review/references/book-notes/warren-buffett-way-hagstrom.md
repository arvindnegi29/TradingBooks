## Framework in the book

Hagstrom combines ideas associated with Benjamin Graham, Philip Fisher and John Burr Williams, then presents Buffett's shift toward buying understandable, durable businesses at reasonable prices. The book organizes the process into twelve tenets (PDF 67–164; printed 41–139):

1. Understand the business and stay within a defined circle of competence.
2. Prefer businesses with a consistent operating history.
3. Look for favorable long-term economics and a durable franchise or competitive advantage.
4. Assess management's rational use of capital, candor, owner orientation and incentives.
5. Analyze financial strength and business performance rather than headline EPS alone.
6. Consider owner earnings: reported earnings adjusted for non-cash charges, necessary capital spending and working-capital needs, recognizing that maintenance capex is an estimate.
7. Compare returns on equity and invested capital with leverage, capital intensity, cyclicality, share issuance and accounting quality in view.
8. Examine margins and the economics that sustain them, not only near-term growth.
9. Ask whether retained earnings create more than a dollar of value for each dollar retained over time.
10. Estimate intrinsic value from a range of future owner earnings/cash flows and discounting assumptions.
11. Compare estimated value with market price and require a margin of safety proportionate to uncertainty.
12. Maintain patient, independent judgment; separate changing business fundamentals from Mr. Market's changing quotations.

The twelve points summarize multiple chapters, not a mechanical scorecard. Business quality, management and financial evidence determine whether a cash-flow estimate is even credible; valuation is conditional on those assumptions. When the business cannot be understood or future economics cannot be bounded, the process permits passing rather than forcing a number.

## Apply as a checklist, not a signal

When the user asks about a long-horizon listed company, structure the review around:

- **Business:** explain the revenue engine, customers, competition, cyclicality, pricing power and reinvestment needs in plain language. State what remains outside the circle of competence.
- **Durability:** look for evidence of sustained economics and a plausible advantage; test how competitors, technology, regulation and customer concentration could erode it. A familiar brand or past high ROE alone does not establish a moat.
- **Management and governance:** inspect current filings for capital allocation, related-party transactions, incentives, dilution, disclosure quality, debt decisions and accounting changes. Treat management claims as claims to verify.
- **Economics:** compare multi-year ROIC/ROE and margins, with debt, leases, buybacks, working capital, sustaining capex and cycle position accounted for. Separate reported cash flow from owner earnings estimates and disclose ambiguous maintenance capex.
- **Value:** use current filings and price, multiple scenarios and sensitivity to growth, margins, reinvestment, discount rate and terminal assumptions. State the date, currency and share-count basis. A margin of safety is only as sound as the assumptions; it is not a universal percentage.
- **Decision discipline:** write the thesis, contrary case, what would falsify it, and the reason to pass. Do not confuse a falling share price with business deterioration—or assume every price decline is an opportunity.

For Indian equities, obtain current exchange/company filings and corporate-action-adjusted data, account for INR and share-count changes, and investigate local governance and related-party disclosures. This 2005 book does not supply current Indian company facts, tax/accounting advice or securities recommendations.

## Transfer limits and counterevidence

- This is a long-term operating-business framework. Bitcoin has no operating company, management, recurring owner earnings or equity claim; do not use this book to derive BTC intrinsic value, price targets, chart levels, entries, stops or technical confirmations. BTC reviews remain grounded in fresh Delta data and the plugin's existing technical/derivatives and risk rules.
- The framework does not map directly to short-horizon options or leveraged trading. “Margin of safety” is not a stop distance, liquidation buffer or position-sizing formula. Do not let patience or a long holding period override the user's horizon, invalidation or available risk budget.
- Discounted cash flow is highly sensitive to assumptions and terminal value. The book's Coca-Cola example is a retrospective illustration using dated company data, not evidence that its inputs were knowable with certainty or that the resulting estimate was correct. Do not copy the 9%, 15% or 5% assumptions.
- Focus investing is controversial and concentration magnifies company-specific losses. The book's suggested portfolio counts and Buffett's historical positions are not plugin defaults. No historical return table establishes a future probability of success; the book's own afterword describes underperformance from copying holdings without matching valuation and growth conditions (PDF 224–234).
- The book's examples are selected in hindsight and its biographical synthesis can simplify attribution. The notes often route through annual reports and secondary sources. Present them as Hagstrom's account, verify claims in current primary documents and include relevant contrary evidence.
- A high-quality company can still be a poor investment at an excessive price, and an apparently low valuation can reflect real impairment or uncertainty. If current filings, business understanding or defensible assumptions are missing, label the view incomplete or pass.
