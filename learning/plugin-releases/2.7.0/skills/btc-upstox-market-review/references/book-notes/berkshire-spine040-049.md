# Source review: spine 040–049 (2003–2007)

All assigned extracted text was read completely, including year-openers and footnotes. These are Pecaut/Wrenn meeting notes and editorial commentary, not a verbatim authenticated meeting transcript. Attribute reported remarks to the named speaker and editorial judgments to the authors. References below are original EPUB hrefs.

## Analytical findings

- **2003 — accounting, incentives, tail concentration** (`index_split_039.html`, “Accounting,” “Option Accounting,” “More Accounting,” “Insurance Risks,” “Correlation,” “Derivatives Warning”): Buffett stresses understanding how reported numbers are assembled, compensation-option economic cost, depreciation, and management incentives. Buffett/Munger warn that small premiums can conceal ruinous liabilities, reinsurer default, unexpected correlation, concentrated counterparties, and optimistic derivative marks. Useful transfer: inspect liabilities, cash commitments and correlated exposure before deployment. These insurance and institutional OTC examples do not validate an options entry signal or make all options identical. Employee-option accounting is not an exchange-traded-options strategy.
- **2003 — selection and patience with a contrary lesson** (same href, “Lifetime Learning,” “Opportunity Cost,” “The Fat Pitch”): reported Buffett/Munger principles favor business value, accumulated knowledge, opportunity cost, occasional strong ideas, criticism and willingness to pay a fair price for quality. The Wal-Mart and See’s examples oppose mechanically refusing a higher price merely because a lower quote was previously available. The long-lived-business/five-year-market-closure thought experiment is fundamentally different from expiry-bound derivatives. Neither concentration nor the betting analogy supplies a retail sizing rule. Author footnotes credit other investors and supply their own acquisition/financing interpretation.
- **2004 — realistic assumptions and survival** (`index_split_041.html`, “The St. Petersburg Paradox,” “Thinking About the Unexpected,” “Last Man Standing”): Buffett/Munger reject implausibly persistent growth assumptions, emphasize rare events and survival through financial contractions, and explain downgrade/collateral feedback. One unmet margin call can end an otherwise successful history. Transfer to trading is a stress-test and liquidity question, not a promise that stops guarantee survivability. The nuclear probability illustration is hypothetical, not an estimated current hazard.
- **2004 — patience, independent thought, incentives** (same href, “Hair Trigger Markets,” “Investment Temperament,” “Errors of Omission,” “Our Own Worst Enemy,” “National Indemnity: Getting the Incentives Right”): cash when alternatives are unattractive, discipline over intellect, resistance to price anchoring, skepticism toward forecasters, and profit rather than volume incentives. NI retains staff through low underwriting volume: an operational illustration of avoiding activity pressure, not a minimum trading frequency. The margin-of-safety implication requires a valuation; it cannot be attached automatically to a chart setup. The footnote describing capital-intensive inflation losers as most technology businesses is the authors’ broad historical gloss, not a Buffett quote or contemporary sector finding.
- **2005 — important disagreement** (`index_split_043.html`, “Dollar Decline”): Buffett favors foreign-exchange exposure while Munger explicitly prefers none; Berkshire has contracts despite the pair’s systemic derivatives warnings. Preserve this disagreement and distinction between particular contractual use and warnings about system leverage/accounting. Do not claim unanimous anti-currency or anti-derivative doctrine. Buffett’s dollar thesis is historical macro judgment; the 2007 chapter later reports carry-cost-driven adjustment.
- **2005 — liquidity and delayed reversal** (same href, “Hair Trigger,” “Correlation of Risk,” “Easy Money,” “Fannie and Freddie and Derivatives”): exit requires another buyer; crowding, easy financing, accounting distortions and correlated subsidiary losses can amplify shocks. Timing remains explicitly difficult. Transfer: a plausible bearish structural thesis is not a timestamp, and exposure across different names can share one risk driver. These passages supply no live evidence about a present instrument or exchange.
- **2005 — pricing, expectations, allocation** (same href, “Pricing Power,” “Incentives,” “Be Out of Step,” “10-Year Returns,” “Long Term, America Richer”): pass-through power, honest reserves, avoiding smooth-earnings pressure, opportunity-led capital allocation and modest valuation-dependent long-term expectations. Historical 6–7% equity expectations, corporate margin concerns, cash amounts, gold comparisons and housing views must retain their date. Long-term American optimism is not a current long signal; no-asset-allocation rhetoric is not an instruction to disregard personal diversification or trading risk budgets.
- **2006 — competence and explicit uncertainty** (`index_split_045.html`, “In/Out/Too Hard,” “Pumpkins to Mice,” “Insurance and Hurricanes,” “Opportunity Cost”): Munger’s too-hard category and commodity-boom admissions support abstention, honest error accounting and no confidence in precisely timing speculative exits. Berkshire nevertheless accepts uncertain catastrophe risk, supported by loss scenarios and a stated cash reserve. This is a contrary case to treating uncertainty as an automatic ban: acceptance depends on economics, expertise and capacity. Its $10 billion reserve is Berkshire-specific, not a universal percentage. Munger immediately corrects Buffett’s exuberant suggestion that distressed buying cannot miss: it can.
- **2006 — fundamentals and changing conditions** (same href, “Ethanol,” “Stick-Built Housing Bubble,” “Coca-Cola,” “Media,” “Derivatives,” “Weaker Dollar: Hard Landing,” “Inflation,” “Low Turnover = Owner Attitude”): assess competitive advantage, lending/accounting quality and deteriorating industry economics; long-duration Coke ownership and low turnover emphasize an owner framework. Derivatives are described as larger but better collateralized, a qualification worth preserving. Macro forecasts, CPI criticisms and commodity/sector claims are period-specific. Author footnotes speculate about investment avenues and praise underwriting; do not pass these off as speaker-verified strategy performance.
- **2007 — ruin, crowding and model limits** (`index_split_047.html`, “Stewardship: Brains Plus Caution,” “Derivatives and the Crowded Trade,” “The Electronic Herd,” “Credit Contractions”): Buffett says a structure alone creates no opportunity, one catastrophic loss defeats many good decisions, derivatives hide leverage, and synchronized automatic selling can worsen forced liquidation. The portfolio-insurance example concerns system feedback, not proof that individual protective stops should be removed. Sigma language and normal-history models may omit endogenous behavior. Preserve trigger/timing uncertainty rather than claiming a predicted crash date.
- **2007 — consequential counterexample to foresight** (same href, “The Subprime Mortgage Mess”): Buffett does not then expect subprime alone to be a huge general-economic drag, conditional on employment and rates; authors voice greater concern. Record the conditional historical statement rather than making the source appear uniformly prescient. The passage itself does not contain later crisis outcomes, so any retrospective result needs another source.
- **2007 — valuation versus mark-to-market risk** (same href, “Buying Businesses,” “Become a Learning Machine,” “Comments on Risk”): returns on capital, cash generation/reinvestment, management treatment, competitive dynamics, competence and value relative to price underpin investing. Munger denies a universal mechanical intrinsic-value formula. Buffett’s rejection of beta as business risk and farmland markdown example does not abolish volatility, gap or margin risk for leveraged traders. Berkshire can tolerate a large catastrophe because of aggregate economics and resources; retail directional exposure cannot inherit those economics. The small real-money learning suggestion is a reported educational opinion, not proof that simulation is useless or an authorization to place trades.
- **2007 — changing macro implementation and delayed bubbles** (same href, “The Declining Dollar,” “Modest Expectations,” “Corporate Profits,” “Private Equity Bubble”): rising carry cost prompts a shift from direct foreign-exchange contracts toward foreign-earning businesses. Locked capital can sustain unattractive conditions; revulsion does not time reversal. Treasury yields, portfolio proportions, tax expectations and valuation opinions are historical. Authors’ footnote about a then-current Bear Stearns fund is their contemporaneous addition, not Buffett’s named example in the meeting remarks.

## Scope and attribution guardrails for a trading skill

These chapters support learning, competence boundaries, economic reasoning, candid uncertainty, loss containment, counterparty/liquidity analysis, incentive awareness and permission to do nothing. They supply no validated intraday edge, strike/expiry choice, Greeks threshold, technical-indicator settings, stop distance, win rate, backtest, execution recipe or expected profitability. Any such operational rule needs separately identified evidence and current instrument-specific inputs. A fresh quote cannot transform a philosophical passage into empirical strategy validation. Reported acquisition anecdotes (including rapid, trust-based diligence) are not transferable instructions to skip present due diligence. Investment concentration and patience presuppose Berkshire’s capital, business knowledge and horizon; time-decaying, collateralized or forced-liquidation positions need different implementation constraints.
