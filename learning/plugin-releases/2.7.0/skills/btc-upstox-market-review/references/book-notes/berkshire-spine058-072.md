## Source-supported principles and limits

- **2012, Chief Risk Officer / Insurance / Systemic Risk / Remembering What Can Go Wrong / Avoiding Mistakes** (`index_split_057.html`): Buffett retains ultimate risk responsibility, focuses on leverage and insurance exposures, maintains cash for severe surprises, and prioritizes surviving to invest another day. Munger criticizes excessive confidence in statistical tail estimates. Transferable to a trading workflow as explicit ownership, adverse-scenario assessment and permission to abstain; this does not supply a risk-per-trade percentage, stop distance, VaR replacement model or derivatives margin formula. The $20 billion reserve is Berkshire-specific and historical.
- **2012, Valuation / Business School / Avoiding Mistakes / Barriers to Entry** (same href): reported Buffett remarks favor business understanding, a five-to-ten-year view, price versus intrinsic value and margin of safety; reported Munger remarks favor existing competitive barriers. These support thesis discipline and refusing unpriceable propositions. Their valuation framework is for ownership of businesses, not evidence for chart signals, intraday mean reversion, option premium selling or timing mispricing corrections. Commentary on long-dated business options and Black-Scholes is not a calibrated alternative pricing model.
- **2012, Special Deals / Float / Shrinking Businesses / Long-Term Outlook** (same href): Berkshire's capital, commitment speed and insurance float are unusual structural advantages; do not imply retail replication. Shrinking businesses can yield cash but the speakers acknowledge their poor early choices. Buffett and Munger differ on plausible future real growth. Preserve uncertainty rather than selecting the more bullish statement.
- **2013, Auto Insurance Primer / Reinsurance Primer** (`index_split_059.html`): Buffett describes credible risk classification and walking away when insurance pricing is inadequate, resisting social proof. Authors' note explicitly emphasizes reliable and sufficiently large data. Useful analogies for data checks and no-trade outcomes, but insurance observations do not validate any market signal. The Peter Lewis redhead anecdote illustrates rash inference from a first loss, not a defensible underwriting rule.
- **2013, BNSF versus the New Normal / Huge Fed Experiment / Effects of ZBIR / Timing / Banks** (same href): macro prediction is repeatedly treated skeptically; both acknowledge unknowns. Buffett nonetheless discusses possible Fed and rate effects, while Munger is less optimistic about returns and banking. Rates influence valuation, but timing rate changes remains unpredictable. Historical low-rate, bank and dollar opinions are contextual observations, not current regime classification or deterministic event-trading rules. Historical success depended in part on competition, period and capital scale.
- **2013, Too Big to Succeed / 1-800-brk-deal / Newspapers / Biggest Threat** (same href): organizational size creates both lower expected growth and crisis-deal advantages. Newspapers are a deliberately cheap declining-earnings case, qualified by Munger as possibly reflecting Buffett's preferences. Munger's criticism of derivative-market social usefulness should remain attributed opinion, not proof all derivatives are forbidden or that one strategy works.
- **2014, Five Things You May Have Missed / Allocation Machine** (`index_split_061.html`): much of this chapter is the authors' interpretation rather than verbatim speaker teaching: equity preference, enthusiasm, the buyback hint, prospective cash generation and the claim of a price floor. Do not present their suggestion that a repurchase authorization creates a floor as an executable guarantee. Capital allocation compares reinvestment, acquisition, debt, dividends and repurchases through long-run opportunity cost. Cash preservation coexists with selective cheap borrowing. The authors explicitly describe Buffett adapting from capital-light businesses to capital-heavy enterprises; a rigid universal preference would misrepresent the source.
- **2015, Filters / Culture / Macro Factors & Predictions / Stock Market and Interest Rates** (`index_split_063.html`): Munger rejects a universal checklist across industries and emphasizes ongoing learning; Buffett uses business-predictability and people filters. These favor context-sensitive reasoning, not discarding concrete operational checks. Valuation depends conditionally on future rates, and the speakers acknowledge prediction failures. No current buy/sell signal follows from 2015 valuation ratios.
- **2015, IBM / Brands / All In / Clayton / 3G / Van Tuyl** (same href): favorable company judgments and Buffett's family concentration are historical and circumstance-specific. The source does not establish that concentration is suitable for a user's leveraged trading account. Buffett's defenses of contested subsidiary practices are his claims in response to criticism, not independent adjudication. Van Tuyl's lack of scale advantage is a useful contrary case to 2013 authors' broad big-company theme. Retain both.
- **Appendix I** (`index_split_064.html`): clearly separates a reproduced Buffett 2014 annual-report account of the 1960s takeover from the authors' subsequent favorable capital-allocation analysis. Buffett calls the emotionally escalated textile commitment a serious error; authors praise later resource reallocation. Lessons: distinguish business quality from apparent cheapness, avoid ego-driven escalation, and recognize salvage success does not prove the initial decision sound. Tax, banking and buyback details are historical mechanics, not current tax/legal guidance.
- **Appendices II–III** (`index_split_065.html`, `index_split_066.html`): popularity and the retail mall illuminate branding, shareholders and Berkshire's change into an operating-business collection. Attendance and sales anecdotes do not constitute evidence for security selection or trading outcomes.
- **Appendix IV** (`index_split_067.html`): five columns are year, investment portfolio in millions, cash, fixed-maturity securities, and equities **and other investments**. The final three are percentages of the investment portfolio, not of all Berkshire assets or a household portfolio. The 1985 cash change is linked to a takeover; the 1998 mix change to the Gen Re merger. Thus allocation shifts cannot simply be treated as market-timing signals. Source rows for 2005 sum to 98% and 2006 to 99%; preserve and flag source arithmetic/rounding uncertainty rather than silently normalize. This table ends in 2014 and cannot substantiate today's allocation. The 2014 authors' discussion relates to then-available 2013 allocation, not the table's later 2014 year-end figure.
- **Appendix V / Acknowledgments / Pecaut & Company** (`index_split_068.html`–`index_split_070.html`): these are curated and lightly edited client newsletters based on the authors' notes, with redundancies removed and some typos preserved; they are not a full verbatim meeting transcript. Acknowledgments identify Daniel Pecaut and Corey Wrenn's authorial roles and client/adviser context. Final adviser registration/contact text is historical book content, not current verification.

## Trading-skill evaluation

A justified addition would be a modest source-labeled principles section: preserve capital, distrust overconfidence, use understandable propositions, distinguish data credibility from an attractive narrative, account for opportunity cost, and accept inactivity. It must be separated from live market analysis and tested trading rules. This reading provides no source validation for BTC/NIFTY intraday entries, volume/open-interest heuristics, options Greeks thresholds, strike selection, liquidity cutoffs, slippage assumptions, position sizing or profitability. Operational rules must have their own provenance and validation. Neither historical return figures nor the authors' admiration establishes future performance.
