# Al Brooks, *Trading Price Action Trends* (copyright 2012)

## Source and review scope

The supplied PDF is a 479-page scan of John Wiley & Sons' 2012 edition (ISBN 978-1-118-06651-5; the upload filename says 2011, and the PDF production metadata is dated October 2011). The complete extracted text was reviewed in page order, including the front matter, introduction, all 26 chapters, author matter and index. The extraction contains about 185,877 words. Chart images are integral to this book: the image inventory identified 121 physical PDF pages with large chart images. Those pages were reviewed in contact sheets, with higher-resolution inspection of representative examples of trend lines, channels, micro channels, horizontal levels and trend patterns. This is not a pixel-level audit of every chart. Physical PDF page numbers and printed book page numbers differ; page ranges below are the printed book pages listed in the contents.

This is Book 1 of Brooks's three-volume series. It focuses on price-action basics and trends. The author explicitly defers trading-range entries, detailed order management and trading math to Book 2, and reversals and best setups to Book 3. Do not fill those omissions with remembered rules and attribute them to this volume.

## Brooks-style chart reading and annotation workflow

### 1. Establish the chart context first

Record the exact instrument, venue, chart type, timeframe, session/time zone, price scale, data source and whether the latest bar is complete. For a BTC perpetual, state the exchange contract and 24/7 session convention. For Indian cash/derivatives, state the exchange session and actual contract. Brooks's examples are mostly U.S. E-mini futures and U.S. stocks with regular-session intraday charts; his Pacific-time opening patterns do not map directly to Delta or NSE sessions.

Start with the chart's current behavior, not a pattern label: trending, ranging or transitioning. Read swing highs/lows, direction of successive swings, overlap between adjacent bars, body size relative to recent bars, tail placement and closes. A large trend bar alone does not prove a new trend. Compare the breakout with its follow-through and the previous regime. Brooks's "always-in" label is a directional summary of his reading, not an objective exchange state; in plugin output call it a tentative directional bias and show contrary evidence.

### 2. Mark levels by source and role

Use distinct labels so a reviewer can tell what each line means:

- **Swing high / swing low:** identify completed pivots from bars visible at the analysis time. Record pivot time and confirmation time; never backfill a pivot before it was knowable.
- **Prior reference:** prior session high, low, open and close; opening-range high/low where relevant; prior breakout point; and signal/entry-bar high or low when that level is part of the setup. Record the session convention and source.
- **Horizontal support/resistance:** mark the price of a visible swing or range boundary Brooks discusses. Label the evidence (number of distinct reactions, dates/times, breakout/test status) and the timeframe. Brooks often draws a line; if real observations form a band, show the band bounds and explain the tolerance from tick size/volatility rather than imply an exact universal zone width.
- **Trend line:** in an uptrend, connect meaningful swing lows; in a downtrend, connect meaningful swing highs. Extend only as a current hypothesis. A line can be re-anchored when new confirmed swings form, but preserve the previous version and timestamp the change. The line break weakens the one-sided trend and raises the chance of a range or reversal; it does not by itself confirm the opposite trend.
- **Trend-channel line:** construct a parallel of the trend line on the opposite side and place it against an intervening swing so the channel reasonably contains the bars, or draw it independently through opposite-side swing points. State which method was used. Brooks treats best-fit/linear-regression lines as less useful for trade decisions than clear swing anchors.
- **Channel/range:** mark both boundaries, slope (rising/falling/flat), anchor swings and whether the channel is parallel, converging or diverging. An upward channel may function as a bear flag in broader context; a downward channel may function as a bull flag. A horizontal channel is a trading range. Treat these as competing interpretations until a breakout and test clarify them.
- **Measured-move reference:** if shown, state the two anchor points and arithmetic, label it as a projected area, and avoid implying that a target must be reached. Brooks says targets are often more useful for planning exits than initiating countertrend trades.

Keep chart labels compact and attached to the relevant anchor: `SH`, `SL`, `prior high`, `prior low`, `opening range`, `TL`, `TCL`, `range high/low`, `breakout`, `test`, `failed breakout`, `candidate`, `triggered`, `invalidated`. Add timeframe, timestamp and source in a legend or table. Keep observed prices, projected levels, entry trigger, stop/invalidation and target separate. Remove stale/duplicative lines from the active view while keeping their audit history; the book warns that too many lines distract from the bars.

### 3. Read the test, not just the touch

A test is price returning to a prior swing, range edge, trend/channel line, breakout point, measured-move area, prior-session reference, or signal/entry-bar level. At the test, describe what price actually does: rejection, pause, close back inside, close beyond, follow-through, or failure to follow through. A one-tick overshoot is not automatically acceptance; a touch is not automatically support/resistance. Require completed-bar confirmation for close-based claims. A breakout that returns inside a range may be a failed breakout; if the failure itself then breaks in the other direction, update the pattern to a failure-of-failure while preserving both events and their timestamps.

### 4. Identify patterns in context

Brooks prioritizes trend/range and recent price action over candle-name recognition. The following is a vocabulary and candidate-detection framework, not a set of validated BTC/India entry rules:

- **Trend bar / doji:** compare real body and tails with recent bars and instrument/timeframe noise; no universal absolute body threshold is given.
- **Reversal bar:** require a meaningful reversal of prior closes/extremes and assess close location, tail, overlap, location and next-bar follow-through. Stronger evidence is required countertrend.
- **Two-/three-bar reversal:** evaluate the sequence as one structure; ensure it is not simply noise within a larger tight range.
- **Inside / ii / iii / ioi / outside:** calculate strict high/low containment from OHLC and name the setup only after bars complete. Treat outside bars as ambiguous because both sides traded through the prior range.
- **H1/H2/L1/L2 and second entries:** label pullback legs and breakout attempts consistently in the chosen context; reset only with a stated rule. These are Brooks's bar-count labels, not ICT/SMC BOS/CHOCH terms.
- **Spike and channel:** identify a strong breakout phase, first pause/pullback, resumed but more overlapping channel, and later channel test. Track nested patterns separately.
- **Wedge / three pushes / final flag / double top-bottom / breakout pullback:** record the swing anchors and whether the pattern is candidate, triggered, failed or invalidated. A visually approximate pattern is not a precise geometric rule.
- **Trend-from-open, small-pullback, trending-trading-range, reversal, trend-resumption and stairs day:** classify only for markets with a valid session/open and enough historical range context. Do not transplant U.S. E-mini time-of-day statistics to crypto's continuous session or NSE without local testing.

Pattern evolution is part of the scan: candidate → trigger/confirmation → follow-through or failure → new pattern or invalidation. Keep the original candidate record; do not rewrite history. If definitions, pivot rules, overlap tolerance, session boundaries or thresholds are not codified, label the scan as qualitative/manual rather than claiming exhaustive automated detection.

## Application to BTC and Indian markets

For a requested scan, begin with Daily/4H context as relevant, then the requested execution timeframes. Brooks himself often preferred a single intraday chart, so multi-timeframe agreement is a plugin-added validation layer, not his rule. For each timeframe report: regime; confirmed swing anchors; horizontal and diagonal levels with provenance; candidate pattern and phase; trigger; invalidation; next test/target areas; strongest contrary evidence; and not-detected/unavailable/not-implemented items. Use fresh venue data and the existing freshness, contract, liquidity and risk gates. Avoid counting overlapping bars, lines and patterns as independent confirmations.

Brooks's volume/institution stories do not establish participant identity or order-flow direction from candles. Price bars alone cannot show which entity bought or sold, whether a stop was deliberately targeted, or whether institutions are on one side. Use actual trade/order-flow data only when its source and aggressor convention are known. Candlestick OHLC is price aggregation, not a complete tape.

## Source limitations and excluded prescriptions

The book is a 2012-era interpretation of mostly U.S. E-mini/stocks and regular-session intraday charts. It predates current crypto derivatives and today's Indian contracts, venues, fees, funding and market structure. The book's claimed breakout/reversal success percentages, trader win rates, institutional-volume proportions, directional probabilities and other numeric assertions are not treated as measured BTC/India facts. Its descriptions of institutions, computer programs, "smart money" and the causes of price moves are narrative explanations, not directly observed from a bar chart. Its zero-sum/efficiency generalizations are not universal across fees, carry, maker/taker incentives and investor horizons.

Do not import as plugin defaults: a fixed 20-bar EMA, U.S. opening-range/time-of-day assumptions, one-tick entries, specific E-mini/stock stop distances, 70%/80%/40% success claims, stock gap behavior, fixed scalp targets, scaling into a losing position, increasing exposure as price moves against a trade, or the author's claim that every move has a particular institutional explanation. Scaling/averaging into adverse movement can increase loss and liquidation risk; it requires a separately validated bounded-risk strategy, and is disallowed by the plugin's default risk discipline. No fixed reward/risk rule or profitable edge is established here.

The book does not cover SMC/ICT labels such as BOS, CHOCH, MSS, FVG, order blocks, liquidity grabs or CRT. Do not rename Brooks's line breaks, tests, failed breakouts, H/L entries or flags as those concepts without the separate operational definition and evidence. This volume contributes a price-action vocabulary and annotation discipline; every BTC/India setup still requires independent validation with realistic fees, spread, slippage, funding, gaps, leverage and out-of-sample regimes. It does not add model weights or authorize orders.
