### The five “fundamental truths” and seven principles

Chapter 8 (printed pp. 125–138) states: anything can happen; an individual need not know the next outcome to make money; wins and losses occur in a distribution for a defined edge; an edge is a probability advantage rather than certainty; and every market moment is unique. Use these as uncertainty and process reminders, not as proof that any setup has positive expectancy. Chapter 11's seven principles can be operationalized as: identify the exact tested setup; predefine invalidation and maximum risk; skip trades whose risk is unacceptable; execute only if conditions remain valid; manage exits by a prewritten plan; monitor rule deviations; never override the risk plan impulsively.

The book's most concrete chart lesson is a cautionary anecdote in Chapter 6 (printed pp. 96–98): an analyst called a support level the day's low, and a large sell order drove price through it. The practical takeaway is that support/resistance is a conditional area where price may react; it is not a guaranteed reversal. A level must be defined from current, timestamped chart data under the plugin's separate chart-annotation procedure, and paired with a clear invalidation condition. The anecdote is illustrative, not a market-impact study.

Chapter 11 discusses a mechanical rules exercise using higher-timeframe context and lower-timeframe support/resistance (printed approximately pp. 191–194). It does not provide a general reproducible method for drawing zones, placing labels, detecting chart patterns, or scanning markets. Do not cite Douglas for detailed support/resistance construction, candlestick geometry, SMC/ICT, indicator thresholds, or a BTC/India detector. Continue to use the relevant charting source notes (for example, Murphy or Bulkowski) for their respective procedures and validate every mark against the actual chart.

## Plugin application

When a signal, entry recheck, or trade review raises execution or behavioral risk:

1. State the observed setup and the rule that makes it eligible. Do not use mindset language to promote an unverified setup.
2. Separate what is observed/calculated from the uncertain next outcome. Show a contrary reading and the condition that invalidates the setup.
3. Before entry, define stop/invalidation, position size from the user's risk budget and actual contract multiplier, and executable costs: spread, fees, slippage, funding/carry and gap/liquidation risk where applicable. A stop is not a guaranteed loss cap.
4. If rules are missing, evidence is stale, size cannot fit the budget, or the user cannot accept the defined loss, mark WAIT / NO TRADE.
5. Review a meaningful, representative, cost-adjusted sample across relevant regimes. Record planned versus actual entries/exits, rule deviations, missed setups, and effect of recent wins/losses. Do not treat the book's 20-trade exercise as proof or adequate statistical power.
6. Keep practice, process adherence and strategy profitability as separate outcomes. A trade can follow the rules and lose; a winner can still be a rule violation.

The book's recommendation of a 3:1 reward/risk ratio is not adopted as a universal rule. Its “risk-free” scaling-out claim is not safe: a residual position remains exposed to reversal, gaps, slippage and execution failure. Its examples are from older U.S. markets, not BTC perpetuals, options or Indian derivatives. Do not assume independent trades, stationary regimes, or ignore fees/funding. Do not import its unsupported claims about “95%” trader failure, intuitive access to hidden market information, collective consciousness, guaranteed ease of profit, or self-sabotage as facts. This source adds no tested edge, market-data source, chart detector, model weight, or trade authorization.
