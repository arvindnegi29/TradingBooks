## Epilogue: “When All Is Said and Done” (PDF 262–271; printed 235–244)

Faith extends the trader's mindset to life: choose one's own path, act despite uncertainty, accept failure as a condition of learning, and update course when reality contradicts expectations. He uses the sunk-cost effect as an analogy for exiting a trade: don't remain committed just because of prior investment. He says money is a poor end in itself and recounts losing liquidity after a thinly traded former-company stock fell; his own selling could move the price, so he sold in small blocks. He says a lower-income consulting period gave him empathy and management experience. These are the author's autobiographical claims and advice, not empirical market rules. There are no substantive chart/table exhibits in this section.

## Bonus chapter: “Original Turtle Trading Rules” (PDF 272–301; printed 245–274)

### System scope and underlying claims (PDF 272–277; printed 245–250)

Faith presents the Turtle approach as a complete, mechanical system covering market selection, position size, entries, protective exits, winning-trade exits, and execution tactics. His central behavioral claim is that a valid system only helps if followed consistently through losing stretches. He says position sizing is the most important element and that insufficient capital undermines diversification because whole-contract granularity prevents comparable risk bets.

The original group traded liquid U.S. futures in the 1980s: U.S. Treasury bonds/notes, coffee, cocoa, sugar, cotton, currencies, S&P, Eurodollar, T-bill, metals, and energy products. Grains were excluded because Dennis already held exchange position limits; meats were excluded due to the author's account of corruption in the pits. Each Turtle could opt out of a listed market, but was told not to trade it inconsistently. This is historical context, not a current market universe recommendation.

### N, dollar volatility, and units (PDF 278–282; printed 251–255)

- True range: `TR = max(H − L, H − previous close, previous close − L)` (equivalently max of the absolute high/previous-close and previous-close/low gaps). The printed display uses the conventional gap-aware range.
- N: 20-day exponentially smoothed true range: `N_t = (19 × N_(t−1) + TR_t) / 20`; seed with the initial 20-day simple average of TR. N is in contract price points.
- Dollar volatility per contract: `N × dollars per point` (contract multiplier).
- One unit: `floor(0.01 × account equity / (N × dollars per point))` whole contracts, so a 1N move is about 1% of account equity for one unit. The example uses $1m equity, heating-oil N=0.0141, $42,000/point and gives 16 contracts after truncating 16.88. At $100k it rounds down to one, showing coarse sizing for small accounts.
- Weekly unit-size sheets were supplied to Turtles. The author says equalizing volatility across instruments improves diversification; it does not make correlated markets independent.

The table is legible and consistent with the explanatory formula; no graphical charts appear in these pages.

### Portfolio caps and equity reduction (PDF 283–285; printed 256–258)

The Turtle limits are 4 units per single market, 6 units in one direction across closely correlated markets, 10 in one direction across loosely correlated markets, and 12 total units in one direction (so theoretically 12 long plus 12 short). Named close groups include heating oil/crude, gold/silver, currencies, and interest-rate futures. The author recounts the post-1987-crash rate intervention: Turtles short rates and lost 40–60% of account equity in one day in many cases; he says caps reduced losses but did not prevent catastrophic drawdown.

Accounts were notional, started at zero P&L with a stated account size, and reset annually based partly on Dennis's subjective evaluation. Drawdown rule: each time equity fell 10% from original account amount, reduce notional size by 20%. Example: $1m → $800k after −$100k; another 10% of $800k loss gives $640k sizing. Faith explicitly says other approaches may be better.

**Table exhibit checked visually:** PDF 283 (printed 256) contains a clear four-row cap table; it matches the prose on PDF 283–284. The extreme-loss story is a caution that nominal unit risk and stop rules do not bound overnight/gap losses.

### Entries, adds, and consistency (PDF 285–289; printed 258–262)

Turtles split allocation between two breakout systems at their discretion:

- System 1: 20-day high/low breakout, one tick beyond the prior 20-day extreme. Ignore the signal if the last breakout in that commodity would have been a winner. The text defines a losing breakout as one where price moved 2N against before a profitable 10-day exit; prior direction is irrelevant and the test includes a breakout that was itself skipped. If a System 1 breakout is skipped after a winner, take the 55-day “failsafe” breakout to avoid missing a large move.
- System 2: take every 55-day high/low breakout, one tick beyond, regardless of the prior breakout result.
- Turtles entered intraday when breakout was crossed; a gap through the level meant entry at the open.
- Initiate with one unit; add one unit each 0.5N beyond the **actual fill** of the prior order, up to the position limits. A slip of 0.5N on an initial breakout shifts the next add to 1N beyond the breakout. Gold and crude examples show four units spaced 0.5N apart; rapid moves could fill four in one day.
- Faith attributes performance differences among Turtles to consistency: he says the best records followed signals and the worst records / those dropped from the program failed to enter consistently. This is the author's retrospective account.

**Example exhibit checked visually:** PDF 288 (printed 261) uses two compact textual price ladders (gold and crude oil), not a graph. Values correctly step by half an N. The entry logic and examples are in text; no other plot/chart in the range conveys data beyond the tables/examples noted.

### Stops and risk (PDF 289–293; printed 262–266)

The standard rule is a precommitted, non-negotiable exit; Turtles did not necessarily leave broker stop orders because they did not want to reveal large positions. Standard stop distance is 2N from entry (N was defined so that 1N per unit is 1% equity, hence nominal 2% risk per unit). On adding units, earlier stops are raised by 0.5N so that in the common case all stops sit 2N from the latest add. Actual gaps/slippage can create different risk. The crude-oil table walks stop levels from first through fourth unit; the gapped fourth-unit example has a different stop from the first three.

Alternative “Whipsaw”: stop each unit at 0.5N (nominal 0.5% account risk), and re-enter at the original entry if price returns there. The author says it had better profitability but more losses/lower win-loss ratio, and a few Turtles used it successfully; no test sample or performance statistics are supplied. At four units, the nominal aggregate stop risk is capped at 2%, with no need to move earlier stops as adds occur.

**Important author-identified inconsistency:** on PDF 290 (printed 263), Faith flags tension with Chapter 10: earlier systems without explicit stops can perform well. He reconciles this by saying moving-average crossings are implicit exits, while fixed stops can help psychologically, especially for beginners. Read this as distinct system designs; the book itself explicitly notes the apparent contradiction.

**Additional sizing ambiguity in the printed rules:** the chapter says no trade could incur more than 2% risk, then defines one unit so a 1N adverse move is 1% of account equity, but standard stops are 2N per unit and the multi-unit stop table on PDF 291–292 moves earlier stops up as adds occur. Applying those printed values literally to the four crude-oil entries (28.30, 28.90, 29.50, 30.10; common stop 27.70) gives aggregate entry-to-stop loss of 5N, or 5% of the sizing equity (before gaps/costs). This differs from a 2% aggregate cap. The book may mean the initial single-unit risk or use a different risk convention, but does not reconcile the arithmetic here. The Whipsaw paragraph explicitly says four 0.5N stops total 2%, which makes the distinction more visible.

**Stop exhibits checked visually:** PDF 291–293 (printed 264–266) contain crude-oil entry/stop ladders for standard and Whipsaw stops. The displayed table values follow the described 2N/0.5N logic; the 30.80 gap example on PDF 292 correctly leaves the fourth unit at a distinct 28.40 stop while previous units sit at 27.70.

Winning exits: System 1 exits all units at a 10-day low (long) or 10-day high (short); System 2 exits at the 20-day opposite breakout. The author emphasizes that many breakouts fail and early profit taking can remove the large trend winners. A 1N winner against a 2N loser would need at least twice as many wins just to offset losses, before costs. The required exit depends on the entry, sizing, and other system components (PDF 294–295; printed 267–268).

### Execution, simultaneous signals, roll, and discipline (PDF 296–301; printed 269–274)

The book advises watching levels and placing orders rather than leaving visible stop orders; it generally favors limit orders near the market to reduce slippage/impact, but acknowledges non-fill risk. For fast markets, it says wait for trading to stabilize / a temporary reversal before placing orders; if the market stabilizes beyond the stop, exit without panic. This execution advice is specific to the historical futures-floor/liquidity context and could miss or worsen a fill in today's electronic markets; it is not a guarantee of improved execution.

When multiple correlated breakout signals arrive, prioritize strongest markets for longs and weakest for shorts; choose one liquid, strong contract month rather than entering multiple months of the same market simultaneously. Strength examples: visual chart assessment, N-adjusted advance since breakout, or 3-month price change divided by current N. Roll only if the farther contract's price action itself would have triggered an existing position; roll before volume/open interest become too low, with timing depending on unit size. These are qualitative tactics, not fully specified ranking/roll algorithms.

Faith closes by stressing adherence and the ability to tolerate long droughts: most yearly profits could come from two or three winners; he says many Turtles failed to make money because they did not follow the rules. He cautions that slowing adds can harm performance by increasing odds that retracement hits stops before the full position develops, and recommends independent historical research, reviewing trade logs and losing periods to establish confidence. These claims lack Turtle cohort results or quantified evidence in this chapter.

## Practical transfer limits for BTC and Indian markets

The chapter supplies a useful **design template** (gap-aware volatility sizing, diversified risk budgets, mechanical breakout/exit rules, explicit execution and roll handling), not a ready-to-run BTC or Indian-market strategy.

- The original markets were U.S. futures with different sessions, liquidity, exchange rules, contract multipliers, and 1980s execution. Recalculate `dollars per point` as the correct INR/USD contract multiplier and tick value for each current NSE/MCX/crypto instrument; account for fees, taxes, funding, basis, margin, lot sizes, expiry/roll, and price bands/circuit rules.
- BTC trades continuously and can gap less in the conventional session sense while still jump violently, with liquidation/funding and venue risk. Indian equities/derivatives have defined sessions, holidays, lot-size changes, expiry structures and regulatory/margin conditions. Daily 20/55-day windows and ATR must define whether bars are calendar, session, or continuous-market bars; daily close and gap assumptions will otherwise be inconsistent.
- N-based sizing targets ordinary volatility moves, not maximum loss. Gaps, limit moves, thin order books, slippage, correlated positions, and stop execution can overwhelm nominal 1%/2% assumptions. The 1987 anecdote makes this limitation explicit.
- Historical caps of 4/6/10/12 units depend on a 1980s portfolio and correlation groups; current BTC, INR FX, Indian index, stock, and commodity exposures need empirically estimated and stress-tested correlations, concentration limits, and instrument-specific liquidity caps. The book's visual strength sorting is subjective and under-specified.
- The 1980s Turtle outcome claims and Whipsaw superiority are not robust current evidence. Before any real capital use, independently test with current point-in-time contracts, survivorship-aware continuous futures/rolls, realistic bid-ask/fees/slippage/funding, out-of-sample and walk-forward tests, and realistic drawdown/liquidation constraints. Nothing in these pages establishes present-day profitability or regulatory suitability.
