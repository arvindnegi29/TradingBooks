## Chapter 10 — systems, test design, and results (PDF pp. 158–176)

Faith argues that historical testing is useful as a disciplined estimate, not a prediction or guarantee. It is the best available way to examine an idea, provided the tester avoids overoptimization and understands the method’s limits (pp. 159–160). He cautions against confusing the ability to run complex statistics with understanding the assumptions and the representativeness of the data (pp. 161–162).

The comparison uses a common portfolio of 26 liquid U.S. futures markets, excluding some highly correlated instruments, and a shared money-management method to isolate system-rule differences (p. 162). Test data run January 1996–June 2006 (p. 163); risk sizing is half the Turtle aggressiveness: 1 ATR is set to 0.5% of equity, and contract count is 0.5% of equity divided by dollar ATR (p. 163). These are historical futures simulations, not BTC or Indian-market results.

The six systems and rules are:

- **ATR Channel Breakout:** 350-day closing-price moving average; upper channel = average + 7 ATR and lower channel = average − 3 ATR; enter next open when prior close crosses outside; exit when close crosses back through average (pp. 163–164).
- **Bollinger Breakout:** 350-day moving average with ±2.5 standard deviations of closes; same prior-close breakout/next-open entry and moving-average close exit (pp. 164–165).
- **Donchian Trend:** 20-day entry breakout and 10-day exit breakout, 350/25-day exponential moving-average trend filter, trades only in the filter direction, and 2-ATR stop (p. 166). The text interprets a breakout as evidence resistance has given way and demand can continue (p. 167).
- **Donchian Trend with Time Exit:** Donchian Trend variant with an 80-day time exit and no stops (p. 167).
- **Dual Moving Average:** 100-day / 350-day moving-average crossover, continuously in the market long or short (p. 168).
- **Triple Moving Average:** 150-day crosses 250-day for entries/exits, with 350-day average as filter; only trade when both faster averages lie on the same side of the long average (p. 169).

**Table 10-1** (PDF p. 171, printed p. 144; visually verified) reports Jan. 1996–June 2006 results. In the table’s order CAGR / MAR / Sharpe / trades / winning % / max DD / DD length: ATR CBO 49.5 / 1.24 / 1.34 / 206 / 42.2 / 39.9 / 8.3; Bollinger CBO 51.8 / 1.52 / 1.52 / 130 / 54.6 / 34.1 / 7.8; Donchian Trend 29.4 / 0.80 / 0.99 / 1,832 / 39.7 / 36.7 / 27.6; Donchian Time 57.2 / 1.31 / 1.35 / 746 / 58.3 / 43.6 / 12.1; Dual MA 57.8 / 1.82 / 1.55 / 210 / 39.5 / 31.8 / 8.3; Triple MA 48.1 / 1.53 / 1.37 / 181 / 42.5 / 31.3 / 8.5. Faith emphasizes that the no-stop time-exit and moving-average systems tested well, and that simpler Dual MA beat the more elaborate Triple MA; he attributes weaker Donchian performance partly to trader effects (pp. 171–172). The text says adding stops to Dual MA worsened all listed measures; Triple MA also worsened on every measure, and Donchian Time was similar except at stops of 10 ATR or wider (p. 172). Figure 10-6 (p. 173) plots Dual MA MAR by stop width and is highest at zero stop, with a sharp decline near 1 ATR; this is a result for this test, not a general argument to omit risk controls.

Faith says trend followers’ drawdowns often come from giving back open profits after trend reversals, rather than simply strings of entry losses (p. 174). Updating through Nov. 2006 changed the rankings and drawdowns: **Table 10-2** (PDF p. 175) gives Dual MA 49.1% CAGR, MAR 1.04, max DD 47.2%, vs 57.8%, 1.82, 31.8% through June; Triple MA likewise deteriorated to 41.2% CAGR and 42.3% DD. Donchian Time remained at 57.1% CAGR and 43.6% DD. **Table 10-3** on the same page shows max-DD increases of 48.4% for Dual MA and 35.1% for Triple MA over that short extension, illustrating how sensitive short historical windows can be. Faith uses this shift to motivate Chapter 11 (pp. 175–176).

## Chapter 11 — backtest failure modes and robustness (PDF pp. 178–204)

Faith names four sources of backtest/live divergence (pp. 179–180): **trader effects** (successful visible patterns attract traders and erode their edge), **random effects** (chance produces unusually good or bad samples), the **optimization paradox** (selecting a parameter raises likely system quality while lowering the historical result’s predictive accuracy), and **overfitting/curve fitting** (excess rules tailored to a small set of historical events).

Trader effects are explained with a hypothetical breakout-front-running example: traders anticipating large stop orders can move price to trigger them, then sell into the triggered demand, changing what the breakout means and the fills/backtest would imply (pp. 180–183). Faith also describes how Turtle orders could be anticipated by floor traders, and his own occasional use of opposite-side “fake” limit orders as a bluff to obscure intent (pp. 183–184). He says crowding can dilute a real edge even without deliberate manipulation, especially in low-edge arbitrage (p. 185).

For random effects, Faith ran 100 random-entry/time-exit tests on the same 10.5-year data. The best returned 16.9% annually (about $1m to $5.5m); the worst lost 20% annually (p. 185). Adding the Donchian trend filter while keeping entries random raised the average return to 32.46% and average DD was 43.74%; across 100 tests, best return/MAR/DD were 53.3% / 1.58 / 33.6%, while worst return/DD were 17.5% / 62.7% (p. 186). On a shorter Jan. 2003–June 2006 sample, 17 of 100 random tests beat the best real-system MAR (1.54), and seven exceeded the Bollinger system’s 52.2% return; the luckiest random run returned 71.4%, MAR 2.07, DD 34.5% (p. 189). The point is that short track records and ranking alone cannot reliably distinguish skill from luck; Faith urges assessing the people and process too (pp. 187–189). His regression-to-the-mean analogy is that extreme outcomes tend to move closer to average over longer periods (pp. 187–188).

Faith defends parameter sensitivity analysis: varying a parameter helps reveal whether performance is robust, random, or overfit; blindly avoiding optimization leaves the trader ignorant (pp. 190–191). In the Bollinger test, he says MAR peaked at a 2.4-standard-deviation channel entry threshold, and a 3.0 threshold returned 28.2% versus 54.5% for 2.4, with the same drawdown (pp. 191–193; Fig. 11-1, PDF p. 192). The 350-day center average is reported as best within the tested 150–500-day range (pp. 193–194; Fig. 11-2, p. 194). For exits, a negative threshold exits long below the center average and short above it; zero is the original center-average exit. The chart’s best MAR is around −0.8, while moving away from that value reduces MAR (p. 195; Fig. 11-3, p. 196).

The optimization-paradox explanation distinguishes in-sample best value B from a typical nonoptimized value A. B may be the better parameter to trade if the future resembles the past, but likely overstates future performance because any future shift away from the fitted peak reduces results; A is less optimal in-sample but less prone to systematic overprediction under small shifts (pp. 197–199; Figs. 11-4 and 11-5). Faith still recommends using good parameter values, while accounting for drift and avoiding the assumption that optimized backtest metrics will recur (p. 199).

Overfitting is excess complexity that helps only a few historically pivotal trades (pp. 199–200). Example: a Dual MA position-sizing rule cuts exposure 90% when DD hits 38%, seemingly improving CAGR from 41.4% to 45.7%, max DD from 56.0% to 39.2%, and MAR from 0.74 to 1.17. But it triggers only once near the end of the test; shifting its trigger by 1 point causes a cliff, including a drop to −0.4% annual return below 37% (pp. 200–203; Fig. 11-6, p. 202). Another optimized rule reduces size 96% for first two days of September and first 25 days of July, apparently lifting CAGR to 58.2% and MAR to 1.48, but these dates reflect particular past drawdowns in only 10 years of data. Faith says the underlying unadjusted system actually returned 41.4% CAGR with 56.0% DD and MAR 0.74 (pp. 203–204). His sample-size point: a rule applying to very few trades/seasons has little evidential basis even if the overall test contains many trades.

## Chapter 12 opening — statistical basis (PDF pp. 206–208)

Faith frames backtests as rough estimates whose usefulness depends on understanding sources of predictive error and basic statistics (pp. 206–207). Statistical inference from a sample depends on both sufficient sample size and representativeness; many trades do not guarantee useful evidence if a rule applies to few events, and the past must be a plausible representative of the future (p. 208). ## Transfer limits for BTC and Indian markets

Treat the book’s numbers as historical futures examples from a specific U.S. portfolio and 1996–2006 market regime. The rules can inform test design—explicit entries/exits, common sizing, out-of-sample evaluation, sensitivity surfaces, and checks for cliffs—but the reported CAGR, MAR, stop conclusions, and chosen lookbacks do not transfer as expected values or prescriptions. BTC trades continuously and has exchange-specific liquidity, fees, funding, leverage/liquidation, and discontinuous venue/regime risks; Indian instruments have their own sessions, contract specs, lot sizes, expiry and roll mechanics, taxes/fees, shorting and regulatory constraints, currency exposure, and liquidity. Rebuild data and execution assumptions for the exact venue/instrument, include realistic costs and gap/slippage treatment, and test multiple regimes and genuinely held-out periods. A no-stop result in this book does not establish that unbounded exposure is acceptable in leveraged or gap-prone instruments.
