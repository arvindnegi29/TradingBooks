## Chapter 12: testing uncertainty and robustness (PDF 209–233; printed 182–206)

The chapter argues that short or unrepresentative backtests can sample only one or two market states and mislead about future performance. It recommends testing across more markets and longer periods, and studying market history. A statistic is “robust” here if small changes to the data do not materially change it; standard CAGR, MAR, and Sharpe are described as especially sensitive to start/end dates and individual trades, with overfitting as a consequence.

- In an example moving a test start by one month and end by two months, Triple Moving Average results move from 43.2% return / 1.39 MAR / 1.25 Sharpe to 46.2% / 1.61 / 1.37; ATR Channel Breakout moves from 51.7% / 1.31 / 1.39 to 54.9% / 1.49 / 1.47 (PDF pp. 211–213; printed 184–186). The author explains endpoint drawdowns alter CAGR’s endpoint-to-endpoint log-equity slope and can also alter maximum drawdown.
- **RAR% (regressed annual return):** annualized return represented by a linear regression (“best fit”) through all equity-curve points, intended to be less endpoint-sensitive than CAGR. The example prose gives RAR 54.67% versus 54.78% across changed test dates, compared with CAGR moving 43.2% to 46.2% (PDF pp. 213–214; printed 186–187).
- **R-cubed / RRRR (robust risk/reward ratio):** RAR% divided by a duration-adjusted average maximum drawdown. The stated construction averages the five largest drawdowns; separately averages the lengths of the five longest drawdowns (days); adjusts average drawdown by average length/365; then divides RAR by that adjusted drawdown. Example: 50% RAR / (25% average maximum drawdown × 365/365) = 2.0. This is intended to include both severity and duration; exact drawdown identification/recovery conventions are not fully specified in this passage (PDF pp. 215–216; printed 188–189).
- **Robust Sharpe:** RAR% divided by annualized standard deviation of monthly returns (PDF p. 216; printed 189).
- Tables 12-1–12-3 compare standard vs robust measures and six systems. The author's reported average sensitivity is CAGR −8.6% vs RAR −1.4%, and MAR −16.2% vs R-cubed −8.0% for the added July–November 2006 test interval (PDF pp. 217–218; printed 190–191). Robust measures are argued to dilute the effect of a single missed/added drawdown and reduce the apparent benefit of rules tuned to a few trades, but they do not eliminate overfitting.
- Representativeness: use more markets and longer histories, including all available data. Sample-size rules of thumb given are <20 observations = large error, >100 more likely to have predictive value, several hundred probably sufficient for most tests; author cautions conventional sample-size formulas do not fit trading data well. Rare rules/events (e.g. bubble endings) cannot acquire large samples; if a rule affected only four trades, there is no sound statistical basis to infer benefit. Generalize rules so they activate more often; avoid single-market optimization and unnecessarily complex systems (PDF pp. 220–222; printed 193–195).
- Practical backtest-to-live exercises: **parameter scrambling** changes selected parameters materially (suggested 20–25%) to see whether performance collapses away from the optimum. In the Bollinger example, moving MA 350 to 250 and exit threshold −0.8 to 0.0 reduces RAR 59% to 58% but R-cubed 3.67 to 2.18. **Rolling optimization windows** optimize on past data then test the following years, repeating at different cutoffs. Table 12-4 reports five ten-year windows; test vs next-period actual differs substantially, especially R-cubed (e.g. 1995–2004: 5.53 test vs 3.90 actual, −29.5%; actual unavailable for 1997–2006) (PDF pp. 223–226; printed 196–199).
- Monte Carlo: trade scrambling randomizes trade order/start dates; equity-curve scrambling samples curve segments. Author prefers equity-curve scrambling because shuffled trades lose calendar clustering/cross-market correlation in drawdowns. He says daily changes alone also lose clustered bad days, and describes using 20-day chunks to preserve autocorrelation in Trading Blox (PDF pp. 228–230; printed 201–203). A Monte Carlo distribution from 2,000 curves shows 90% exceeded 42% RAR in the example, but is only as credible as the source backtest and cannot cure optimization bias (PDF pp. 231–232; printed 204–205). Figure 12-3 was visually checked: it plots a gray RAR% distribution and a cumulative confidence curve, with the 90% confidence marker around 42%; it communicates uncertainty, not a forecast guarantee.
- Chapter conclusion: backtests are rough approximations; even robust metrics cannot justify a precise promised future performance. Sales claims of a specific expected level are treated with skepticism (PDF p. 233; printed 206).

## Chapter 13: building robust portfolios (PDF 234–248; printed 207–221)

The chapter rejects choosing a single “best” backtest on historical CAGR/MAR. The distribution of market conditions can change: a system that did best in mostly quiet trends may lose its relative advantage if future trends are more volatile. The examples of six-state sequences are explicitly too small to infer a reliable cycle. Robustness is framed around **diversity and simplicity**: simple, adaptable rules depend on fewer specific market behaviors; portfolios should combine many sufficiently uncorrelated markets and systems so different components can perform under different conditions (PDF pp. 234–240; printed 207–213).

- Adaptability: filter trades when a system’s favored market state is absent. Example: Donchian Trend’s portfolio filter avoids breakouts against the trend, which the author says occur when conditions are less favorable; simpler durable rules are said to be less tied to observed quirks than layers added during development (PDF pp. 238–240; printed 211–213).
- Market diversification: seek markets with distinct opportunities; adding near-lockstep interest-rate products adds little diversification. Foreign markets may diversify and can be manageable for systems using close/open data. The author warns against excluding markets solely for weak short-sample performance because trends can be rare and diversification benefit may outweigh standalone profitability (PDF pp. 240–242; printed 213–215).
- Coffee anecdote and exhibit: author estimates a missed March 1986 coffee futures Turtle trade would have used N = 1.29 cents and 103 contracts/unit for a $5m account, four units = 412 contracts; at ~$34,000/contract, ~$14m or 280% on account. Figure 13-1 (visually checked, PDF p. 242; printed 215) charts coffee rising from about 140 to 280, with entry 141.91 on 1985-10-10, initial stop 139.16, exit 234.37 at 1986-01-16 open after 65 bars. This is a hindsight simulation and the author explicitly says he did not take it; he then explains Dennis excluded coffee due to liquidity and the combined Turtle/Rich size was thousands of contracts near viable volume limits. The episode does not establish that illiquid markets should be traded.
- Liquidity is the primary exclusion criterion: thin markets can leave even a one-contract order behind hundreds of contracts with no opposing side and are more exposed to large unexpected price shocks (rough rice, lumber, propane examples) (PDF pp. 242–243; printed 215–216).
- Three market classes proposed (PDF pp. 243–245; printed 216–218): (1) fundamentals-driven (currencies, rates; macro/policy influence, author says most liquid/clean trends/easier for trend followers); (2) speculator-driven (stocks and futures such as coffee, metals, crude; perception-driven/harder); (3) aggregated derivatives (e-mini S&P example; index averaging dilutes momentum, hardest for medium-term trend following). Author argues markets behave similarly within class over the long run and says he excluded class 3, while other Turtles traded it. “Trader memory” explanation: recent collective memory of the 1978 gold/silver bubble made later moves choppy; by 2006 the old trend was less salient. This is an author hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal result.
- Capital constraint: author estimates at least $100,000 for reasonably diversified long-term futures trend following (still too high a risk level for many); 10 markets may be more erratic than 50–60 (PDF p. 246; printed 219). System diversification example: two Bollinger Breakout legs, long and short volatility breakouts, individually RAR/R-cubed 38.2%/1.19 and 14.5%/0.41, combined 61.2%/5.20; author attributes improvement to low/negative correlation. He estimates $200,000 for adequate long-term diversification and $1m+ for 4–5 systems (PDF pp. 246–248; printed 219–221). These capital estimates are era- and contract-specific.
- Closing claim: a mature program spanning many systems and markets, with adaptability or simplicity and no dependence on one market condition, is more likely to perform consistently than a highly tailored small portfolio (PDF p. 248; printed 221).

## Chapter 14: psychological execution (PDF 250–260; printed 223–233)

The author argues that a teachable rule set only works if traders follow it. Ego can drive traders to seek complexity, secret knowledge, prediction, and validation; discretionary wins feed ego, while excessive leverage can turn short-lived gains into ruin. He contrasts this with systematic execution and says humility helps accept uncertainty, losses, and simple methods (PDF pp. 250–256; printed 223–229). The Ping-Pong tournament story illustrates how a superior player’s need to prove dominance made him over-aggressive under pressure while Faith’s defensive adaptation helped him win; he connects that person’s ego and “secret” explanation to inconsistent Turtle trading. This is anecdotal illustration, not empirical proof of a general trading-psychology mechanism.

Faith recounts his own insensitive bragging about holding four units, then resolves to consider how actions affect others (PDF pp. 256–257; printed 229–230). He calls consistency central: a plan has no value unless executed. He suggests building confidence through simulation and checking assumptions against market data, while warning paper trading differs from risking real capital; he admits his own psychology was unusually suited to consistency and disclaims expertise in treatment of psychological problems. He notes his experience is mainly trend following and says other trading styles may fit different temperaments (PDF pp. 257–259; printed 230–232).

The chapter’s four “Lessons from the Turtles” (PDF pp. 259–260; printed 232–233): (1) trade with positive expectation, (2) manage risk so one can continue trading, (3) execute consistently, and (4) keep systems simple because simple systems tend to hold up better. Final admonition: commit to the first step and act on the plan.

## Transfer limits for BTC and Indian markets

The durable transfer is methodological: test across regimes and venues, retain out-of-sample/rolling checks, measure sensitivity to parameter and sample changes, account for correlated drawdowns, size risk so losses do not end participation, and execute consistently. Diversification should be assessed by measured joint returns and liquidity rather than labels alone.

The book’s numeric claims and implementation details do not transfer directly. Its examples are mostly exchange-traded futures/stocks and data through 2006; its systems are medium/long-term, and its 20-day block scrambling choice is an author-specific calibration. BTC trades continuously across venues, with spot/perpetual/futures differences, funding, liquidation mechanics, fragmented liquidity, exchange outages and changing fee/margin rules. Indian market access, product availability, taxes, and regulation are time-sensitive; the book cannot establish present eligibility or costs. BTC is one underlying, so spot, perpetuals, and correlated crypto products should not be counted as independent markets without return-correlation and stress-liquidity evidence. RAR/R-cubed can be tested as candidate diagnostics, but published formula detail is incomplete for exact replication, and neither should replace live-cost, gap/slippage, drawdown, and position-limit analysis.
