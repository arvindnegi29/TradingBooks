## Main rules, formulas, and claims

- **Regime framing (printed pp. 26–27; physical 53–54):** Figure 2-1 divides markets into stable/quiet, trending/quiet, stable/volatile, and trending/volatile. Trend followers are said to favor trending conditions; quiet trends are easier to hold because profits are less prone to sharp givebacks, while volatility makes trend-following psychologically harder. Countertrend traders are associated with stable but volatile ranges; swing traders can use volatility in either trending or non-trending markets. The author says traders should read indications of the current state rather than predict direction; state characteristics can overlap and change over time.
- **Core Turtle principles (printed pp. 29–39; physical 56–66):** trade a positive-expectation edge, manage risk to survive losing periods, execute consistently, and keep the method simple enough to catch trends. Edge and expectancy are framed in repeated-trial terms; single-trade outcomes should not be used to judge decision quality. Losses are a cost of operating a tested method, not automatically proof of a bad decision.
- **Risk of ruin and position sizing (printed pp. 31–33; physical 58–60):** the dice example illustrates that a favorable game can still wipe out an oversized bettor. Ruin risk rises disproportionately as per-trade bet size rises. Turtles split positions into units and scaled contracts by market volatility so daily dollar fluctuations were roughly comparable. Their volatility measure **N** is identified with average true range (ATR). This is a qualitative description here; the exact historical N calculation and sizing mechanics are not fully specified in this span.
- **Risk/expectancy arithmetic (printed pp. 36–37; physical 63–64):** the text defines system expectation as average dollars won per trade divided by average dollars risked, and says expectation 0.2 means about $0.20 per $1 risked over time. Risk is entry-to-stop distance × contracts × contract multiplier. Example: long gold at $350, stop $320, 10 contracts, 100 oz/contract gives $30 × 10 × 100 = $30,000? The source itself prints **$3,000**. This is an apparent arithmetic error: under the stated units, $30 × 10 × 100 = $30,000, unless the quoted price convention or multiplier was intended differently. This matters because the example is offered as a risk calculation; do not reuse the printed result without checking contract quoting conventions.
- **Breakout method (printed pp. 38–39; physical 65–66):** buy when price exceeds the highest price over a lookback, sell short below the lowest low. The author calls the historical intermediate system System 1 with 20-day highs/lows and the longer System 2 with 60-day highs/lows. Entry reference levels were recalculated after each close. A stop was described as no farther than 2N from entry; the book says this “also happened” to equal 2% of account because position size was N-based. Do not treat 2N as inherently equal to 2% equity: that relationship depends on the unit sizing and account-risk convention. The second exit type is not detailed in these pages.
- **Historical execution example (printed pp. 40–45; physical 67–72):** trainees initially practiced with three-contract units, maximum four units (12 contracts), and maintained a trade log. Faith describes buying February 1984 heating oil on a breakout, scaling to 12 contracts, holding through a roughly $0.98-to-$0.94 retracement, and exiting at $1.03 near expiry after Dennis’s office said not to take delivery. Figure 3-1 marks entry $0.8451, initial stop $0.8377, exit $1.0310, entry date Jan. 11, 1984, and exit Jan. 30 after 13 bars. The narrative says his account rose $78,000 and that other trainees either entered smaller or exited during the pullback. This supports the execution/discipline lesson, but is one retrospective case, not a performance estimate.
- **Probabilistic mindset (printed pp. 47–62; physical 74–89):** the author argues to judge execution over a series, not by whether one trade wins; think in probabilities, avoid recency bias and prediction, learn from but do not ruminate on past trades, and take responsibility for execution. Table 4-1 shows 17 consecutive losing cocoa breakout trades before a large short winner in November 1998; the table reports the first 17 at a combined loss of $16,750, then winning trades and a cumulative total of $55,903. It illustrates a trend-following return distribution with many small losses and relatively few large gains. Figure 4-3 shows a positively skewed monthly-return distribution for a simplified Donchian Trend test; Figure 4-4 shows the R-multiple trade distribution, with 3,746 losing trades versus 1,854 winners, most losses near 1R and a long right tail (43 trades at 10R or more, as described in the prose). An **R-multiple** is trade profit divided by initial risk; the example uses $1,000 initial risk and $5,000 profit = 5R.
- **Historical method caveat (printed p. 61; physical 88):** Faith says he personally used “100 percent” of his account allocation on a longer-term **10-week breakout** system. This does not match the immediately preceding description of the historical Turtle System 2 as 60 days (12 weeks). It may describe a distinct implementation, but this excerpt does not reconcile the difference.
- **What constitutes a system edge (printed pp. 63–73; physical 90–100):** edge is a repeatable statistical advantage sufficient to overcome commissions, slippage, data, and other costs. The author identifies portfolio selection, entry signals, and exit signals as three system components; entries and exits should be paired with a compatible style. For entry testing, **MAE** is maximum adverse excursion and **MFE** maximum favorable excursion over a chosen horizon. Figure 5-1 diagrams favorable/adverse movement. Average MFE greater than average MAE is presented as evidence of positive entry edge; random entries should be near parity.
- **E-ratio formula (printed p. 67; physical 94):** for a selected post-entry horizon, compute each signal’s MFE and MAE; divide each by ATR at entry; average normalized MFE and MAE separately across signals; divide average normalized MFE by average normalized MAE. E5/E10/E50 indicate horizon in days, including entry day. Random long/short-at-open tests over the stated sample averaged E5=1.01, E10=1.005, E50=0.997. A 20-day Donchian breakout with a 50-day/300-day moving-average trend filter was tested on 28 high-volume U.S. futures from Jan. 1, 1996 to June 30, 2006: unfiltered E5=0.99, E10=1.0 and E70=1.20; with the filter, E70 was reported as 1.33, while 70,000 random filtered entries yielded E70=1.27. The text says the filtered curve reaches roughly E120=1.6. Figure 5-2 shows the unfiltered E-ratio starting below 1 and rising around/above 1 over longer horizons; Figure 5-3 shows a smoother, higher curve with the trend filter. The stated implication is that breakout entries can be poor over the short horizon yet advantageous over the medium/long horizon, and a trend filter can improve selection.
- **Exit assessment (printed p. 73; physical 100):** exits cannot be isolated from the entries and position conditions that activate them; evaluate exits by their effect on whole-system performance, not by post-exit price movement.
- **Support/resistance and biases (printed pp. 75–77; physical 102–104):** the chapter opens by attributing support/resistance to anchoring, recency bias, and the disposition effect. Figure 6-1 marks repeated levels on a coffee futures chart (May–Nov. 2000). The example says a recent low near $1.13 anchors perceptions; a later revisit around $1.15 feels cheap relative to that anchor and can attract buyers, helping explain bounces at prior highs/lows. The excerpt frames this as a behavioral tendency, not a deterministic price rule.

## Source inconsistencies and limits

- **Clear arithmetic mismatch:** printed p. 37 (physical 64) multiplies a $30 price difference by 10 contracts and 100 ounces, but prints $3,000; the stated multiplication yields $30,000. Contract quote/multiplier conventions could explain a different economic risk, but the printed arithmetic does not.
- **Lookback mismatch:** printed p. 61 calls Faith’s longer-term breakout 10 weeks, whereas printed p. 38 calls Turtle System 2 60 days/12 weeks. The excerpt does not explain whether the personal system differed.
- **Test-length descriptions vary:** printed p. 56 calls the simplified Donchian Trend monthly-return test 20 years; printed p. 59 calls the trade-distribution test 22 years. They may be separate test windows, but no dates reconcile them in this range. The E-ratio sample is separately dated 1996–mid-2006.
- **Scope and evidence:** the “edge” measurements are historical, in-sample summaries from selected/high-volume U.S. futures and 1996–2006 data; no uncertainty intervals, transaction-cost sensitivity, robustness across subperiods, or current-market validation are given here. The text’s behavioral mechanisms and Turtle stories are explanatory claims, not proof that a present-day signal will retain edge.

## Practical transfer limits for BTC and Indian markets

The transferable ideas are testable process choices: express risk in volatility units, cap portfolio exposure, use explicit entry/exit rules, model costs, and evaluate distributions over a sufficiently broad set of trades. The exact historical lookbacks, 2N stop, 50/300 filter, and reported edge ratios should be treated as hypotheses for fresh testing, not plug-in parameters.

BTC trades continuously and has venue-specific spot/perpetual prices, funding, liquidation mechanics, and abrupt volatility changes; Indian instruments also have distinct contract multipliers, lot sizes, trading sessions, liquidity, fees, and market-access constraints. A transfer test must use the exact instrument and venue, contract specifications, realistic order fills/slippage, fees and (for perpetuals) funding, session/gap behavior where applicable, and portfolio correlations. For Indian markets, validate applicable current product and access rules with authoritative current sources before implementation; this book does not address them. Position sizing should use instrument-specific point value and ATR in account currency, then check leverage, margin/liquidation distance, concentration, and drawdown at the portfolio level. Because all cited validation is old U.S. futures evidence, neither its numerical results nor its behavioral explanations establish a live BTC or Indian-market edge.
