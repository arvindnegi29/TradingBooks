## Material covered

### Foreword, preface, and introduction (PDF 12–27; printed xi–xxvi)

Van Tharp presents Faith as the most successful Turtle, claiming he made Dennis over $31 million while trading Dennis’s account; he reports Faith earned $78,000 in the initial training period, nearly three times the others, despite shared rules. Tharp attributes different results chiefly to psychology and position sizing, and argues the book’s value lies in execution, behavioral finance, game theory, sampling, and robust system evaluation rather than a “magic” system. Treat these as Tharp’s framing and claims, not independent verification.

Faith describes the Turtle project as Dennis and Eckhardt’s test of whether trading could be taught. The introduction says more than 1,000 applied, 40 were interviewed, 13 were ultimately chosen, and another 10 were selected for a follow-on cohort the next year. It reports an average return above 80% per year over four and a half years. Faith says he joined at 19, had already programmed 30–40 historical system tests, and had more confidence in systematic trading than peers. After two weeks of training, the first group began with small accounts and a one-month trial; Dennis varied allocations after assessing results, giving Faith a $2 million account, the largest. Faith says concepts are easy to learn but hard to apply consistently.

Faith says this book recounts his own experience rather than a comprehensive group history, and its preface claims he earned more than 100% returns for the four-plus years of the program. It says the book will discuss transfer to stocks and Forex. These are retrospective author claims and should not be read as evidence that the system works unchanged in other markets or later periods.

### Chapter 1 — “Risk Junkies” (PDF 28–38; printed pp. 1–11)

The chapter distinguishes investors, who buy businesses/assets for long-term value, from traders, who trade financial instruments and take or transfer risk. Its central market-role distinction is **liquidity risk** (being unable to transact at a desired price; spread-making/scalping and arbitrage) versus **price risk** (material price change; hedgers transfer it and speculators assume it). It describes hedgers, scalpers/market makers, and position traders as interacting participants. An illustrative pound-futures transaction follows a corporate hedge through a floor scalper to a speculator; the example is explanatory, not a current venue or contract recommendation.

Quantitative examples and mechanics in range:

- A stock quote of $28.50 bid/$28.55 ask has a $0.05 spread (PDF 29–31; printed pp. 2–4).
- The historical CME British-pound contract example is 62,500 pounds with a $0.0001 minimum tick, worth $6.25 per contract. Ten contracts therefore move $62.50 per tick; a two-tick spread is about $125 gross, described in the text as “just over $100” (PDF 32–34; printed pp. 5–7). Fees and execution risk are not modeled.
- A scalper holding 50 contracts through a 50-tick adverse move loses $15,625: 50 × 50 × $6.25 (PDF 36; printed p. 9).

The price-action account is that prices change when buyers or sellers alter the prices at which they are willing to transact; large orders, inventory pressure, panic exits, and triggered orders can reinforce movement. The author describes pit trading as the then-current context, but says electronic execution was replacing pits and lowering costs. This 2007 description and its forecasts are historical; the participant roles remain a conceptual explanation.

### Chapter 2 — “Taming the Turtle Mind” (PDF 40–52; printed pp. 13–25)

The chapter argues that human emotion and systematic judgment errors influence price action and create opportunities, while also making rule-following difficult. It lists eight biases: loss aversion, sunk-cost effect, disposition effect, outcome bias, recency bias, anchoring, bandwagon/herd effect, and belief in the law of small numbers. Illustrations include the claim that losses can carry roughly twice the psychological force of gains; a planned $1,000 loss that grows to $2,000 and potentially 30–50% account damage as a trader avoids realizing it; dot-com investors anchoring to prior highs; and the warning that four wins in six tests or three years of fund outperformance are too little evidence for strong conclusions. These are behavioral illustrations, not calibrated parameters for any market.

Trading styles described:

- **Trend following:** seeks multi-month moves, entering around historical highs/lows and exiting after a sustained reversal. Faith says effective methods tend to have similar performance characteristics; trends are infrequent, with 65–70% losing trades described as typical. Choppy periods and reversals can cause extended drawdowns. He offers a rough heuristic that trend-system drawdowns may approach the level of annual returns (e.g. 30% expected annual return and a 30% drop from peak), and says wide entry-to-stop distance demands enough capital. This is a broad author heuristic, not a dependable forecast or sizing rule.
- **Countertrend:** sells/shorts near new highs on the premise most breakouts fail; the book later attributes its profit mechanism to support and resistance.
- **Swing trading:** shorter trend-style moves, with examples of three- or four-day holds and 5-minute, 15-minute, or hourly charts.
- **Day trading:** flat before the close to reduce overnight-news exposure; can use short-horizon trend/countertrend methods. **Scalping** seeks the bid–ask spread; **arbitrage** exploits temporary price discrepancies across related markets. The example buys Comex gold at $550 and sells five CBOT Globex e-mini gold contracts at $555 (PDF 51–52; printed pp. 24–25).

Faith introduces four market states: stable/quiet (narrow range), stable/volatile (large daily/weekly changes without major months-long change), trending/quiet (slow drift without severe retracement), and trending/volatile (large moves with significant shorter reversals). He says trend followers prefer trending/quiet markets. PDF 52 contains the four-state list and this preference, but no displayed chart; it refers to Figure 2-1, which is beyond the requested physical-page range.

## Source inconsistencies and limits

- The foreword (PDF 14; printed xiii) recounts that ten people “would be selected” from forty applicants, while Faith’s introduction (PDF 21; printed xx) says 13 were ultimately chosen and 10 more joined the follow-on program. This may be planned versus actual/first cohort, but the book does not reconcile it explicitly.
- The foreword (PDF 13; printed xii) describes a screening test of 63 true/false plus 11 short-answer questions. Faith’s introduction (PDF 22; printed xxi) says he took Richard’s 40-question test. These may be separate tests/stages; the text does not say so, so the distinction is ambiguous.
- Faith’s introduction reports more than 1,000 applicants, 40 interviewees, 13 first-cohort selections, and 10 next-year selections; Tharp’s foreword says forty were interviewed from over a thousand and “only a portion” selected. The latter is compatible but less specific.
- The book’s trading examples and market microstructure are dated to 2007 and rely heavily on U.S. futures/pit-era examples. The Southwest oil hedge, quoted prices, contracts, exchange locations, tick economics, and execution assumptions are time- and venue-specific. The examples should not be treated as current contract specifications.

## Practical transfer limits for BTC and Indian markets

The transferable material in this range is behavioral and conceptual: define the hypothesis and holding period, distinguish liquidity/spread capture from directional price risk, account for the other side’s incentives and forced flows, and avoid changing a tested process because of a small run of outcomes. The pages provide **no BTC entry/exit rules, BTC sizing formula, or Indian-market trading rules**.

Do not port the quoted U.S. futures tick values, contract sizes, pit execution assumptions, spread capture, or generic 65–70% loss-rate/30% drawdown statements directly to BTC or Indian instruments. BTC markets trade continuously across fragmented venues and derivatives can add funding, basis, liquidation, and venue/custody risks; Indian cash and derivatives have their own session hours, contract lots/ticks, fees, margin and regulatory rules, and can gap when closed. Any application needs current instrument specifications, realistic fees/slippage/funding, liquidity and market-impact checks, and testing on the intended venue and timeframe. The four “market states” are descriptions only here; no classification algorithm or validated regime trigger is supplied in this page range.
