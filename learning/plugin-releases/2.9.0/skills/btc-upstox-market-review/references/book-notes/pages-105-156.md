## Findings by chapter

### Chapter 6 — support/resistance and price instability (physical 105–111; printed 78–84)

The author explains support and resistance partly as a self-reinforcing belief: traders anchor on recent highs/lows, take profits near prior highs, and buy near perceived support. The disposition effect (locking in gains) can create resistance. These are tendencies, not hard rules; reactions can occur before/after a marked level or fail entirely.

For countertrend systems, a bounce from prior highs/lows is the proposed edge. For trend-following systems, the failure of those levels is the edge: traders cling to the old anchor, while a break can trigger trapped longs to sell and accelerate a move. The book defines a breakout as a move through prior resistance/support; Turtles buy resistance breakouts to enter long, sell support breakdowns to enter short, and use opposite short-term breaks to exit.

The “points of price instability” idea is that near a support/resistance boundary, one side may soon prevail and the market may move away; an invalidating level can be relatively close, so the cost of being wrong may be small. The illustration suggests a countertrend long at $2.10 with a stop 6¢ below, versus a trend-following short at $2.10 with a stop 5–6¢ above. These are examples, not universal thresholds. The text urges quick exits when the expected bounce/break fails.

### Chapter 7 — measuring risk and reward (physical 112–134; printed 85–107)

Risk is broader than volatility or drawdown. The four stated risks are drawdowns, low returns, price shocks, and system death (previously profitable logic ceases to work). The author stresses that drawdowns can exceed tested history and that traders often abandon systems under emotional pressure. A Donchian Trend example is presented with 43.7% CAGR and a 38% historical drawdown (Jan. 1996–May 2006); a later update shows a 42% drawdown after a new trader starts, illustrating that prior backtests do not cap future losses.

The 1987 episode illustrates overnight gap risk: the author describes a $20m managed account down about $11m after eurodollar futures gapped up more than two points, with gold/silver also adverse; a separate Donchian system example shows a 65% drawdown from the shock, roughly twice the prior test’s indicated drawdown. Stops cannot guarantee an exit at their stated prices through gaps or market closures/limits.

Suggested standalone measures: maximum drawdown (peak-to-trough percentage loss), longest drawdown (time from peak to a later equity high), standard deviation of returns, and R-squared / equity smoothness. Reward measures include CAGR, average rolling one-year return and average monthly return; inspect equity curves and the distribution and sequence of monthly returns as well as summary ratios.

The Sharpe ratio is described as excess return over the risk-free rate divided by return standard deviation. The author argues it is useful for comparing traditional, unleveraged stock mutual funds but incomplete for futures/alternative funds because it does not capture trading-style, concentration/diversification, leverage/exposure, and manager-track-record confidence risks. Smooth returns can hide tail risk and leverage: LTCM and Amaranth are examples; short-option strategies can produce steady returns while remaining vulnerable to price shocks.

The MAR ratio is annual return divided by maximum drawdown; the Donchian example has CAGR 27.38% and month-end drawdown 22.35%, MAR 1.22. Using peak-to-trough daily data instead gives drawdown 27.58% and MAR 0.99. The point is that month-end sampling can understate drawdown. The author recommends combining measures and warns that historic ratios do not predict future values. Strategies with attractive historic ratios can attract capital, crowd trades and erode their edge; trend following’s unpopular drawdowns may help its longevity, though returns are cyclical.

### Chapter 8 — risk and money management (physical 136–148; printed 109–121)

Money management is framed as keeping ruin risk acceptable while preserving profit potential, by sizing trades and capping aggregate exposure. Risk tolerance is personal; aggressive returns greatly increase blow-up risk. The text recommends conservative sizing, especially during early trading, and suggests targeting a simulated drawdown no more than half of one’s believed tolerance to leave room for worse-than-seen history and shocks.

Contract sizing should normalize for volatility and contract value. Example: natural gas ATR represents over $7,500 per contract; a 2-ATR stop implies about $15,000 exposure per contract, 30% of a $50,000 account, so the author says zero contracts would be prudent at that account size. A $1m account would still face 1.5% per contract on that example. These are illustrative historical contract values, not current specifications.

The Turtle “N” / unit logic: use dollar volatility (1 ATR move) to size each market comparably. A unit is the number of contracts for which 1 ATR represents 1% of account equity; for $1m, that is $10,000, divided by the dollar value of 1 ATR per contract. The author recounts using a 1/2-ATR stop where typical Turtles used 2 ATR; because an overnight gap can skip stops, stop-distance-only sizing would have made his position four times larger and exposed him to fourfold loss. Volatility-based sizing kept comparable shock exposure despite the different stop distance.

Portfolio constraints described for the Turtle program: at most 4 units per market; 6 units across highly correlated markets; 10 units in one direction, raised to 12 only if positions included uncorrelated markets. The author says these limits both constrained correlated shock exposure and filtered out later-signaling, lagging markets. Examples: among four interest-rate futures they often held only the first two signals; among six currency futures, usually only two or three. He calls unit limits integral to the system, not an optional detail.

For risk estimation, replay major historical shocks over 30–50 years against likely positions, using simulation to estimate losses/drawdowns or ruin; also test more severe hypothetical scenarios. A historical sample is not a worst-case guarantee.

### Chapter 9 — trend-following building blocks (physical 150–156; printed 123–129)

No indicator works all the time; tools only improve odds that a trend has begun/ended. The chapter surveys: (1) breakouts above/below a lookback high/low (the original Turtle system’s primary tool), with shorter windows tending toward shorter trends and longer windows toward longer trends; (2) moving averages; (3) volatility channels around a moving average; (4) time-based exits; and (5) simple lookbacks comparing current price to an earlier price, optionally offset by volatility (example: buy when current price exceeds the price 100 days earlier by 2 ATR).

A 20-day/70-day EMA crossover example identifies an early-June long entry when the shorter EMA crosses above the longer. Volatility channels can signal a move when price crosses an average plus/minus a volatility allowance; time exits can reduce time spent in a stalled trend; simple lookbacks offer a low-complexity alternative. The closing advice is to use basic building blocks and avoid searching for an overfit “perfect” indicator. Different tools can often be tuned to react at similar speeds, so elaborate indicators may add curve-fitting risk without durable advantage.

## Transfer to BTC and Indian markets

The transferable content is conceptual: define a reproducible breakout/exit rule, size exposure using volatility and contract multipliers, cap correlated aggregate exposure, inspect daily/intraperiod drawdowns, and stress test gaps, liquidity failures and clustered losses. For BTC, ATR sizing should be computed in the traded contract’s quote currency with the correct lot multiplier and then translated into account currency; spot, linear/inverse perpetuals and options have different payoff and liquidation behavior. BTC’s 24/7 trading reduces exchange-close gaps but does not remove jumps, funding/margin liquidations, venue outages, order-book slippage or correlated crypto selloffs.

For Indian markets, use current NSE/BSE/commodity contract specifications and tick/lot/margin rules before translating the book’s futures examples. INR denomination, exchange price bands/circuit limits, session gaps, expiry rolls, liquidity, taxes/fees and broker/exchange margin treatment can change realized risk. Cross-market “correlation groups” should be measured for the actual portfolio and stress periods rather than copied from the book’s 1980s currency/rates markets. Position caps and ATR units are useful design ideas, not ready-to-run BTC or Indian-market settings. The book supplies neither an empirical BTC/India validation nor current instrument parameters.

- Physical pages 135 and 149 are explicitly blank; printed pagination jumps over pp. 108 and 122, as described above.
- Physical p. 119 says “returns of 30 percent per trade,” while the examples immediately compare annual returns and three-year CAGR; this appears to be an editorial wording error for “per year.”
- The p. 119 three-year sequence (5%, 5%, 100%) compounds to 2.205x and a CAGR of about 30.2%, so “same ... 30 percent” is approximate, not exact.
- Physical p. 138 describes Figure 8-1 as drawdowns “as risk levels increase”; the chart suggests a generic simulated relationship, but gives no test settings or return path. The sharp 100%/ruin threshold is not portable to other systems.
- Physical p. 140’s NG ATR/dollar value is explicitly “earlier this year” relative to the 2007 book; it is not a present-day contract quote.
- The book discusses 1987, 2001 and 2006 cases and uses market conventions of its publication era. Historical examples and contract limits should not be treated as current trading rules or guarantees.
