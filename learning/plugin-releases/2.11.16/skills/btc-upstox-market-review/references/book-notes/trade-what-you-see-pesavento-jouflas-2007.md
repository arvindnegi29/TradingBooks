## What the chart examples teach

### Marking and labeling sequence

1. Fix the instrument, venue, session, timeframe, price scale and completed-bar cutoff. The source examples span historical U.S. equities, indexes, futures and intraday sessions; they do not define current Delta or Upstox data conventions.
2. Mark visible completed swing highs and lows and classify the current structure as an uptrend (higher highs/higher lows), downtrend (lower highs/lower lows), range/contraction, or expansion/transition. Keep the chart's time and price axes explicit. Do not force symmetry where it is absent.
3. Select the relevant impulse/correction pivots before measuring. Label the specific pattern points (A-B-C-D or X-A-B-C-D), preserve anchor timestamps, and distinguish observed pivots from a projected D/completion zone. A pattern remains a candidate until the source's structural conditions are met and the market confirms or invalidates it.
4. Plot retracements from the named swing in a consistent direction; label the anchors and ratio. Treat levels as overlapping price zones, not tick-perfect lines. The book's primary levels are .618, .786, 1.00, 1.272 and 1.618; it also discusses .382/.50 mainly for trends, .707/.886 in AB=CD variants, and 2.00 for larger extension. It repeatedly says ratios are not exact and must be combined with structure and current regime.
5. Add repeated-swing measurements only after observing common move sizes on the same instrument/timeframe. The authors describe cloning a line to compare swings, including nested smaller swings. A harmonic is a measured historical rhythm/zone, not a fixed instrument constant or forecast. Re-estimate it from a defined rolling sample; do not import the book's 2007 S&P, euro or commodity point/tick values, nor its 3%-of-price shortcut.
6. For a live plan, mark the possible trigger, the invalidation/failure point, the protective stop rule, target zone(s), and the contrary scenario separately. The chart examples often use partial exits and move stops after a first target; that does not make a remaining position risk-free. A gap can jump a stop.
7. Review a higher timeframe first, then a lower timeframe for a possible entry/refinement. Record opposing lower-timeframe patterns and do not switch a losing day trade into a position trade after entry. For scans, report timeframe and coverage; label pattern candidates, not fully validated detector signals.

### Geometric and harmonic vocabulary

The authors frame price movement as alternating contraction/range and expansion/trend, and look for visually balanced swing geometry. Symmetry includes price length, slope and sometimes bar/time count; exact equality is not required. Long-range bars, gaps and accelerating legs can warn that a projected completion may extend or fail. Their “harmonic numbers” are recurring swing sizes observed in a given market and timeframe; they are combined with Fibonacci retracements and named structures as confluence, not as independent evidence.

### Pattern recognition rules summarized from the text

- **AB=CD** (Chapter 4, pp. 39–54): impulse AB, corrective BC (often a .382/.50/.618/.786 retracement), then CD toward a measured or extended completion. D should pass B; BC cannot exceed AB (a full retrace/double top-bottom is allowed). A fast/steep CD, a gap, or a wide-range bar can imply extension (the source discusses 1.272–2.00+); slope and elapsed bars matter. The projected D is a zone and may be overshot.
- **Gartley “222”** (Chapter 5, pp. 55–68): X-A-B-C-D, with AB=CD inside the larger X-to-D structure. D must not exceed X; B must not exceed X; C cannot exceed A (equal/double top-bottom is described as rare but possible). It is a retracement/retest candidate, not proof of a major reversal. The note should flag that the book repeats an approximately 70% historical success claim without supplying a reproducible, cost-adjusted test in this edition.
- **Butterfly** (Chapter 6, pp. 69–83): extension/reversal candidate with D beyond X, commonly near 1.272 or 1.618 of XA and an AB=CD inside the structure. The authors warn of fast failure if extension continues; their 2.618 negation reference is not a validated BTC threshold. Watch symmetry, thrust from C, gaps, wide bars and risk to a failure point. Do not countertrend-trade a strong move without a capped plan.
- **Three Drives** (Chapter 7, pp. 85–94): three successively higher highs or lower lows, intervening retracements, an AB=CD component, and roughly symmetric price/time legs; 1.272/1.618 extensions are the book's reference points. It can end a swing or produce a correction rather than a full reversal. The source lists same-direction gaps/oversized extensions as caution and says a forced-looking pattern is suspect.
- **Retracements and multiple timeframes** (Chapter 8, pp. 95–115): simple two-leg pullbacks to Fibonacci zones and the distinct historical “opening price retracement” stock setup. The latter's asserted >60% statistic and stock-only premise are not current verified facts; exclude it as a BTC/Indian signal. Read higher timeframe first, use lower timeframe for a defined entry, and respect contrary structures.
- **Classical patterns** (Chapter 9, pp. 117–134): double tops/bottoms, Head and Shoulders (neckline break, measured height or extension target), and five-point broadening formations, with Fibonacci relationships used as optional measurement. Mark the pivots, neckline/boundaries, failure level and any projected measured move explicitly. A failed breakout can produce a move in the opposite direction; a drawn shape alone does not establish one.
- **Trend days** (Chapter 10, pp. 135–150): the source's historical S&P cues include a narrow-range prior session, a gap that holds, early wide-range bars, directional higher highs/lows or lower highs/lows and cross-index agreement. It describes shallow/repetitive pullbacks, flags/consolidations and tests/retests of higher-timeframe Fibonacci levels. These are research hypotheses for other instruments, not portable thresholds or a live BTC detector.

### Risk, confirmation and workflow

Chapter 11 (pp. 153–164) treats risk and execution as central. Warning signs include a gap in CD, unusually wide bars near completion, tail closes at completion and a steeper CD leg. Confirmation examples include a tweezer, a gap in the intended direction, strong directional bars, or waiting one completed bar. These are context clues, not guaranteed reversals. The source advocates defining risk before entry, using protective exits, reducing size when a stop widens, tracking total open risk, and not increasing a preplanned stop to avoid a loss. It gives 1–3% account-risk examples and historical contract values; do not adopt those percentages or old multipliers as the user's risk settings. Size only from the user's budget and current actual product terms/costs.

Chapters 13–14 support a pre-trade chart plan and review log: define instrument/setup, trigger, invalidation, size, stop, target and management; record intended versus actual execution, costs and outcomes; audit winning/losing streaks and rule deviations; keep before/after charts and scan notes; prepare for events and platform/order failure; use a repeatable premarket/during/after-market checklist. The 20-sample exercise is a process drill, not statistical proof. Any strategy claim needs adequate out-of-sample, cost-adjusted tests across regimes.

Chapter 12's long-option examples are historical. It discusses underlying movement, strike/moneyness, expiry/time decay, volatility, dividends, rates and open interest, and shows both an option gain and one that expires worthless. Do not repeat its “85% expire worthless” figure as a current universal statistic. Analyze current Greeks, IV/skew, spread, depth, expiry, funding/carry and payoff; a premium-limited buyer can still lose the full premium. Never translate the book's historical option prices or contract multipliers.

## Transfer limits and source quality

This 2007 volume focuses on U.S. stock and futures examples. Its patterns and ratios are educational hypotheses, not evidence of a profitable BTCUSD perpetual or Indian cash/derivatives setup. Fibonacci levels, pattern labels and visual symmetry can be subjective; multiple ratios derived from the same pivots are correlated rather than independent confirmations. The book does not provide reproducible scan code, BTC/India validation, current execution terms, or a complete empirical study with survivorship, lookahead, fees, slippage and funding addressed. Historical S&P harmonic values, options statistics, opening-price performance claims, chart examples, margins and contract specifications must not be presented as current market facts.
